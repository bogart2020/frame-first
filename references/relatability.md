# Relatability and sharing

Why a line makes a viewer feel seen, and what makes a post worth forwarding. `hooks.md` owns the
opening; `slop-patterns.md` owns the generic test; this file owns the middle and the caption.

Every rule below traces to a named study. The evidence, its sample sizes, and its limits are in
`research/2026-08-20-sharing-psychology.md`. **Where the evidence stops, this file says so** —
a labelled bet is useful, an unlabelled one is a lie.

## The one rule

**Write the line the viewer would say about themselves, not the line you would say about the
footage.**

Sharing is not enjoyment. What predicts a share is whether the content is **self-relevant** and
**socially relevant** — whether forwarding it says something the sender wants said about them, to
the person they send it to. [Established: fMRI linked to 117,611 real shares, replicated
preregistered across two countries — Scholz et al. 2017 *PNAS*; Chan et al. 2023 *PNAS*. Word of
mouth serves impression management and social bonding, and is "predominantly self-serving" —
Berger 2014 *JCP*.]

Everything else in this file is a way of executing that rule.

## Aim the sentence at the viewer, not at the world

A line the viewer has to check against their own memory is processed differently from a line
that describes a category. This is the mechanism under "relatable," and it is a fifty-year-old
memory finding, not a vibe. [Established: meta-analysis, Symons & Johnson 1997 *Psychological
Bulletin*; replicated with fMRI, N = 83 and N = 54, 2025.]

| Describes a category (semantic) | Forces a check against the self (self-referential) |
|---|---|
| "Everyone's phone struggles in low light." | "You've got forty photos from that night and you can't tell which one is your friend." |
| "Parking at these things is a nightmare." | "You know the exact second you gave up and just took the ticket." |
| "It's hard to enjoy a show while filming it." | "I watched the whole encore through a five-inch screen and I still don't know what the last song was." |
| "Small venues have better atmosphere." | "The whole room knew the words to a song that has 11,000 plays." |

**The test:** does the line require the reader to retrieve a memory in order to parse it? If it
can be understood without one, it is a description.

**The failure to avoid:** a line so general that everyone technically qualifies — "we've all been
there" — is not self-referential, it is the absence of a referent. Recognition needs a *specific*
situation the reader either has or hasn't been in. Narrow beats broad here, every time.

## Say it in first or second person, about one occasion

Three moves, in order of how much they change a line:

1. **One occasion, not a habit.** "the encore last Tuesday," not "encores."
2. **A person in it** — you, or the viewer. Not "one" and not "people."
3. **A detail that fixes it in time** — a number, an object, a physical action.

Concrete language is judged **more probably true than abstract language carrying the identical
claim**, mediated by perceived vividness. [Established: four experiments, Hansen & Wänke 2010
*PSPB*.] And of everything a storyteller controls, only two things reliably increase absorption:
an **imaginable** plot (ρ = .29) and **verisimilitude** — believability, "the likelihood that
story events may actually happen" (ρ = .27). [Established: meta-analysis of 132 effect sizes,
van Laer et al. 2014 *JCR*.]

**What this does not license.** Specific detail buys **belief** and **absorption**. It does not
reliably buy persuasion or generosity — the classic "vividness effect" mostly failed to replicate
(Taylor & Thompson 1982), and the "one victim beats a statistic" effect failed a preregistered
N = 1,004 replication (Maier et al. 2023). So never argue that a detail is *moving*. Argue that
it is **believed**, and that its absence marks the writer as someone who wasn't there. That is
the claim `slop-patterns.md` is actually making.

## Name one emotion, and pick it from what the evidence supports

Discrete emotions predict sharing. Valence does not, and **arousal does not** — a head-to-head
comparison of all three theories on 4,000+ annotated posts found the discrete-emotion model won.
[Established: Paletz et al. 2023 *Science Advances*.] Do not rank emotions by intensity or
"activation"; that theory failed to replicate at N = 111 and N = 160. [Prowten et al. 2024
*Psychological Science*.]

| Emotion | Evidence that it drives sharing | Use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Amusement** | Strongest cross-medium support: Facebook posts, video ads, and lab | Freely |
| **Being moved / heartwarming** | Facebook sharing (Paletz); construct validated across 19 nations | Freely. This is the reunion, the stranger's kindness, the crowd carrying a line |
| **Inspiration**, **warmth**, **excitement** | Two independent field studies of **video** sharing plus a holdout sample (Tellis et al. 2019) — the best-matched medium available | Freely |
| **Recognition ("this is exactly my experience")** | **No direct sharing study exists.** Inferred from self-referential encoding + the self-relevance input to share-value | **The plugin's central bet. Label it as a bet, act on it anyway** |
| **Surprise** | Video ads (drama element); one archival study (+14%) | Yes |
| **Awe** | One archival study (+30%). The awe literature itself is strong but measures prosociality, not sharing | Yes, without the confident theory |
| **Anger / injustice** | One archival study (+34%), but **exactly null** in randomized headline experiments, and the famous moral-language result did not survive re-analysis | Only when it is genuinely the story. Never manufactured |
| **Sadness** | **Contested.** One 2008 email study says it suppresses sharing; two larger later studies (one randomized) found the opposite | Do not avoid it on the strength of the old finding |
| **Nostalgia** | **No sharing study found.** What is established is that nostalgia works through **narrative** — it needs a story about a transition, not just an old object | Use, but build it as a story |
| **Curiosity** | Owned by `hooks.md`. An opened gap that never closes reads as a tactic and **costs** shares | Only with the close attached |

**One dominant emotion per piece.** Two named emotions is a piece that decided nothing.

## Utility earns saves, not sends

A technique, setting, or fact is a real Reward — it is not a Share driver. In video specifically,
**information-focused content significantly reduces sharing**. [Established: two independent field
studies plus a holdout sample, Tellis et al. 2019 *Journal of Marketing*.] This is why a genuinely
useful post can show high saves and near-zero sends, and why that is not a defect
(`references/arrrsr.md`, and the diagnostic table in `ff-strategy`).

**If a send is the goal, pair Utility with a second value type.** The technique plus the moment it
cost you. The setting plus what you got wrong first.

## What reads as a tactic, and what it costs

The mechanism is specific and worth stating, because it explains why bait is *worse than neutral*
rather than merely ineffective: a reader who infers **manipulative intent** derogates the
**source** — judges the author less competent and less trustworthy — and then doesn't share.
[Established: controlled experiment plus 19,386 articles across 27 publishers with Twitter share
data; clickbait articles got **far fewer** shares, with large effect sizes. Mukherjee, Dutta &
De Bruyn 2022 *JAMS*.]

Note what the same study found in the other direction: **high-emotional headlines were shared
more.** The problem is never emotional intensity. It is visible engineering.

| Reads as a tactic | The same idea, without the tell |
|---|---|
| "Tag someone who needs to see this 👀" | Nothing. If the send test is passed, the send happens; if it isn't, asking won't fix it |
| "Wait for it…" | Name what is coming and stay for the reason it is worth waiting: "the lighting guy did something at 0:09 I've never seen anyone do live" |
| "This changed everything for me." | "I stopped bringing the second lens after this night." |
| "Comment your thoughts below!" | A real unresolved disagreement: "I think the amber grade is worse. Convince me." |
| "Nobody talks about this." | "I've asked four people who shoot these and none of them do this, so I might be wrong." |
| "POV: you're at the best show of your life" | "POV: it's song four and you've just realised you're going to be filming this instead of watching it" |

**The false-intimacy version of the same failure.** Manufactured closeness — confiding a feeling
the footage does not support, "you guys," an addressed vulnerability that costs nothing — is the
same inference of engineered intent, applied to a relationship instead of a headline. There is no
study measuring this specifically; it is grouped here because the mechanism is the same one and
the plugin should treat it the same way. **Labelled: this row is an extension, not a finding.**

## Recurring format beats one interesting post

Interesting things get **immediate** word of mouth but not **ongoing** word of mouth. Things that
are **cued by the environment or publicly visible** get both — right away and over months.
[Established: 300+ products, field experiment and lab experiment with real conversations, Berger
& Schwartz 2011 *JMR*.]

A recognizable recurring format is a cue. This is the evidence behind the **Relationship** stage
in `arrrsr.md`, and the reason `ff-package` logs hook architectures — a format the audience can
recognize is an asset; the same shape four posts running is a liability
(`data/hooks-used.md`).

## Checklist before a caption ships

1. **Send test.** Who forwards this, to whom, and what does sending it say about them? Not
   "someone might like this."
2. **Self-reference test.** Does at least one line require the reader to retrieve their own
   memory to parse it?
3. **One emotion named**, from the table above, and it is the one the footage actually carries.
4. **One detail only presence supplies** (`slop-patterns.md`) — for belief, not for effect.
5. **No visible tactic.** Nothing that a reader could name as a move.
6. **If Utility is the only value type**, either accept it will be saved rather than sent, or add
   a second.
