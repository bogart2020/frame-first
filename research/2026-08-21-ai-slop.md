# AI-slop research — 2026-08-21

Research pass only. No skill, reference, or script was changed. Every claim below carries the
source that owns it, its venue, sample, and method. Claims that reached only secondary or
unmeasured sourcing are labelled **folklore** and are not promoted to fact anywhere in this file.

Verification environment: macOS 25.6.0, `bash` 3.2 / `zsh`, GNU-style `grep`/`awk` present but
**`grep` on this machine resolves to `ugrep`** — every regex in §4 was re-checked for BSD-`grep`
portability and the notes say where that matters.

## Two problems, kept separate

The brief is right that these are different problems and this file keeps them apart throughout:

| Problem | Target | What optimizing for it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **P1 — the ChatGPT feeling** | A human reader's impression that nobody was there and nobody chose these words | Remove measured over-represented vocabulary; break formulaic structure; add witness detail |
| **P2 — detector evasion** | A classifier's score | Raise perplexity, vary sentence length artificially, inject errors |

**The plugin should serve P1 only.** §6 gives the measured reason: the published detectors
mis-flag non-native English writers at rates above 50%, so a gate tuned to detector output would
punish this creator for writing the way they write. P2 is out of scope and should be stated as
out of scope in the doctrine.

## Conventions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Measured** | A named study with stated corpus, N, and method reports the number. |
| **Named-by-detectors** | Expert human annotators in a peer-reviewed study cited this cue. Not a frequency measurement, but not folklore either. |
| **Folklore** | Repeated widely; no measurement located. Includes every "10 words that scream AI" listicle. |
| **Bad writing ≠ LLM** | The phrase is weak or clichéd but there is no evidence LLMs use it more than humans. Belongs in `slop-patterns.md`, not in an AI-tell deny-list. |

| Kobak, González-Márquez, Horvát, Lause — *Delving into LLM-assisted writing in biomedical publications through excess vocabulary* | **Peer-reviewed**, *Science Advances* 11(27), 2025; [10.1126/sciadv.adt3813](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adt3813); [arXiv 2406.07016](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07016) | **>15M PubMed abstracts, 2010–2024** | Excess frequency vs. a counterfactual extrapolated from pre-2023 trend; words whose 2024 frequency exceeds the counterfactual | **900 excess words** identified, of which **407 are "style" words** (268 verbs, 53 adjectives, 46 nouns, 23 adverbs). Both are the real thing the brief asked for. The second one publishes its data, which is what makes
§2 possible: the repository [berenslab/llm-excess-vocab](https://github.com/berenslab/llm-excess-vocab)
ships `results/excess_words.csv` (the annotated 900-word list) and `results/yearly-counts.csv.gz`
(a 362,442 × 15 matrix of how many abstracts per year contain each word).

**Method used for every ratio in this file.** I downloaded `yearly-counts.csv.gz` on 2026-08-21 and
computed, per word, `(count_2024 / N_2024) / (count_2022 / N_2022)`, using the count of abstracts
containing "the" as the per-year denominator `N` (1,359,148 in 2022; 1,435,517 in 2024). 2022 is
the last full pre-ChatGPT year; 2024 is the last year in the data. **This is my computation, not
Kobak et al.'s published statistic** — they report excess-vs-counterfactual, which is stricter. My
ratio is a cruder proxy that also absorbs ordinary topic drift, so treat it as an ordering signal,
not a published effect size. Every ratio below is reproducible from that file in three lines.

### 1.5 The register bridge — LLM writing outside science

The PubMed corpus is a formal scientific register and is a **bad proxy for Instagram captions**
(§9 says so plainly). The nearest bridge measured with the same α method:

> Liang et al., *The widespread adoption of large language model-assisted writing across society*,
> ***Patterns*** (Cell Press), 2025; [arXiv 2502.09747](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09747);
> [journal](https://www.cell.com/patterns/fulltext/S2666-3899(25)00214-4). Corpora: 687k US
> consumer financial complaints, 537k corporate press releases, 304 US job postings-corpora and
> 15,919 UN press releases, Jan 2022 – Sep 2024.
> By late 2024: **≈18%** of consumer-complaint text, **up to 24%** of corporate press releases,
> **just under 10%** of job postings (small firms), **≈14%** of UN press releases estimated
> LLM-assisted. Growth **plateaued** during 2024.

Consumer financial complaints are written by ordinary people, unedited, emotionally charged — the
closest published measurement to informal first-person writing. It confirms the phenomenon reaches
that register. It does **not** give a caption-register word list. No study measuring LLM lexical
shift in Instagram/TikTok captions was located (§9).

### 1.6 Where the over-representation comes from (mechanism, for the doctrine)

- Juzek & Ward, *Why Does ChatGPT "Delve" So Much?*, **COLING 2025** (peer-reviewed);
  [arXiv 2412.11385](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.11385). Identify **21 focal words** whose uptick is
  likely LLM-caused. They explicitly **fail to find evidence** that architecture, algorithm choice,
  or training data explains it; RLHF is the surviving candidate, and their own participant study is
  inconclusive on it.
- Juzek & Ward, *Word Overuse and Alignment in Large Language Models: The Influence of Learning
  from Human Feedback*, **BIAS 2025 workshop @ ECML PKDD** (peer-reviewed workshop);
  [arXiv 2508.01930](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.01930). Follow-up finds participants
  **systematically prefer** text variants containing the focal words — i.e. the overuse is
  a learned reward-model preference, a "misalignment" between annotator taste and user taste.

**Consequence for the plugin:** the over-represented words are words *raters found more
impressive*. That is exactly why they read as effortful-but-empty. It also predicts the list will
drift as models retrain — so `ai-tells.txt` must carry a review date.

### 1.7 Punctuation: the em-dash claim IS measured

The em-dash discourse is usually folklore-shaped, but there is real measurement:

| Source | Status | Corpus | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Em-ergence of the em-dash*, [arXiv 2606.29540](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29540) | **Preprint, but pre-registered** (OSF HFT8C; frozen plan with 6 supporting, 6 sensitivity analyses, 2 falsification tests) | **69,632 medRxiv preprints**, first versions, 2020–2025, Discussion sections ≥500 chars | Em-dash prevalence in Discussion sections **4.23% → 11.58%** pre- vs post-ChatGPT; **+7.35 pp** (95% CI 6.94–7.77); **OR 2.96** (95% CI 2.77–3.17). Effect **absent in boilerplate sections**. Authors state it is a **population-level indicator, not a per-document detector** |
| [Piece of K, *The Rise of the Em Dash in Ecology Abstracts*](https://www.pieceofk.fr/the-rise-of-the-em-dash-in-ecology-abstracts/) | **Blog analysis, method stated** — qualifies under the brief's rule | 10,000 English ecology abstracts from OpenAlex, 600–3,500 chars, 2021 vs 2025; frequency normalized by total character count | Em-dash relative frequency **more than doubled**; no other character came close. Question marks, ampersands, semicolons **declined**; colons, commas, asterisks flat |
| [SlopDetector, *Is the Em Dash an AI Tell?*](https://slopdetector.org/blog/em-dash-ai-tell-data) | **Blog; do not rely on** | Human baseline = 702,939 words of **19th-century public-domain literature** (Melville, Twain, Thoreau, Dickens, Austen); AI figures **taken from a third party**, prompts and sample sizes not described | Reports GPT-4.1 10.62/1,000 words vs "human" 3.23. Rejected as a baseline: 19th-century novelists are not a contemporary-human control, and the AI numbers are unreproduced |

**Verdict on the em-dash:** measured and real at population level, in *formal* registers, and
explicitly not a per-document detector. For a two-line Instagram caption, *one* em-dash carries
almost no information. See §4.3 for what to do instead — and note the repo has a live bug here
(§4.3.1).

### 2.1 Three live defects found by running the gate

All three verified by executing `scripts/slop-check.sh` on 2026-08-21. None of them is about `ai-tells.txt`
content; they are gate-mechanics bugs found while auditing it, and they matter more than any single
word on the list.

**Defect 1 — the gate flags the creator's own best caption.**

```
$ echo "this song hits different when you know theyre never coming back" > own.md
$ ./scripts/slop-check.sh own.md
SOFT  AI tell: "hits different"
-- hard: 0  soft: 1 --
```

That line is recorded in `data/voice.md` as posted to Instagram with the best traction on that
platform, next to an explicit note: *"'hits different' is deliberately **not** on this list — it
appears in the creator's own best-performing Instagram caption below. Don't let a generic AI-tell
list overrule a confirmed real example."* The voice file says don't; `ai-tells.txt` does. One more
soft hit anywhere in the same draft and this trips a FIX verdict on a proven caption. By the
hardening doc's own standard — *a gate with false positives stops being trusted* — this is a
defect, not a preference.

**Defect 2 — the creator's em-dash rule does not actually fire.**

```
$ echo "the lights went down — and then it started" > emd.md
$ ./scripts/slop-check.sh emd.md
-- hard: 0  soft: 0 --
VERDICT: surface clean.
```

`data/voice.md` lists `em dash (—)` under *Words and phrases I would never use*, so the HARD stage
extracts the literal string `em dash (—)` and greps for **that whole string**. A caption containing
an actual em-dash never matches. The soft structural check only fires above **3** em-dashes, which a
two-line caption will never reach. Net effect: the creator's own hard rule is unenforced. Fix is in
§4.3.1 and requires no change to `voice.md`.

**Defect 3 — the `"chills" / "goosebumps"` entry never fires either, for a different reason.**

```
$ echo "straight up chills the whole set" > chills.md
$ ./scripts/slop-check.sh chills.md
-- hard: 0  soft: 0 --
VERDICT: surface clean.
```

The extractor's `gsub(/^"|"$/,"")` strips only the *outermost* quotes, so the bullet
`- "chills" / "goosebumps"` becomes the single literal `chills" / "goosebumps` — a string no
caption will ever contain. Any `voice.md` bullet listing **two alternatives separated by `/`** is
silently dead. Of the 8 deny-list entries currently in `voice.md`, **two of eight are unenforceable**
(this one and the em-dash). The fix is to split on ` / ` and treat each side as its own literal,
which also needs the quote-stripping applied per-side rather than per-line. Same file, same loop as
§4.3.1; worth doing in one pass.

## 3. Recommended `ai-tells.txt` replacement

The file is read by `slop-check.sh` line-by-line with `[[ "$phrase" == \#* ]] && continue`, so
**comment headers are free** — the list can be grouped and dated without touching the script. That
is the only structural change proposed.

```
# Universal AI-writing tells. One fixed string per line, matched case-insensitively.
# SOFT signals: presence is a prompt to look, not proof of slop.
# The creator's own deny-list in data/voice.md is what hard-blocks.
#
# EVIDENCE BASIS — reviewed 2026-08-21, review again 2027-02 (models retrain; this list drifts).
#   [M] Measured over-representation in a named corpus study. See research/2026-08-21-ai-slop.md §1.
#   [D] Named by expert human detectors as a cue (Russell et al., ACL 2025).
#   [A] Assistant artifact - not a corpus finding, but diagnostic of chat output pasted into a caption.
```

## 4. Structural checks implementable in bash

Constraint honoured: **bash + `grep` + `awk` only, no Python.** Every snippet below was run on
2026-08-21 against `evals/fixtures/good.md`, `evals/fixtures/slop.md`, the seven real captions
transcribed from `data/voice.md`, and a hand-built adversarial slop caption. Results are reported,
including the ones that failed.

### 4.0 The finding that governs this whole section

**Sentence-based metrics do not work on this creator's captions, and the reason is measurable.**

Splitting on `[.!?]` over the creator's seven real captions yields **2 "sentences"**, one of them
64 words long — because the Kodaline register is *all lowercase, no ending punctuation*, which
`data/voice.md` records as a deliberate voice choice. `evals/fixtures/good.md` yields 3 sentences.
Both are far below the ~8-sentence floor any variance statistic needs.

```
real captions      sentences=2   -- too few to judge
good.md            sentences=3   -- too few to judge
adversarial slop   sentences=10  mean=5.0  sd=1.48  cv=0.30
```

So: **burstiness, perplexity proxies, and hedging density are all out of scope for captions.**
They become viable only for the long-form drafts `ff-package` produces (YouTube descriptions,
multi-paragraph scripts). Every check below is marked with the input length it requires.

### 4.1 Tricolon / rule-of-three — **implement**

Evidence: Russell et al. (ACL 2025) list "consistently listing exactly three items" under Sentence
Structure, their **#2 cue at 35.9%** of expert explanations. `slop-patterns.md` already names
"tidy tricolons" but nothing enforces it.

```bash
if grep -qE '[A-Za-z]{3,}, +[A-Za-z]{3,}, +[A-Za-z]{3,}[.!?]' "$DRAFT"; then
  echo "SOFT  structure: three-item run closing a sentence (tricolon)"; soft=$((soft+1))
fi
```

Tested:

| Input | Fires? | Correct? |
|---|---|---|
| `Bold, beautiful, unforgettable.` | yes | ✅ true positive |
| `I brought one body, one lens, and a spare battery.` | no | ✅ (the `and` + short words break it) |
| `Security pulled me out at song four, I shot the rest one-handed, and it shows.` | no | ✅ |
| `he played it slower, quieter, sadder than the record` | no | ✅ (no sentence-final punctuation — and this is a *good* human tricolon, so the miss is welcome) |
| 7 real captions | no | ✅ |

**FP risk: low.** The `{3,}` length floor and the required terminal punctuation are what do it.
The check deliberately misses unpunctuated tricolons; that is the right trade for this creator.
Do **not** relax it to allow a trailing `and` — that variant fires on ordinary human lists.

### 4.2 The "it's not X, it's Y" antithesis — **implement, extended**

The existing regex is `it'?s not [a-z' ]{2,40}, it'?s`. It misses the common variants. Evidence
status is honest: the *construction* is named by expert detectors as `not only … but also`
(35.9% cue category); the specific `it's not X, it's Y` framing has **no located measurement**
(§9) and is supported only by mechanism arguments in blog posts. Treat it as [U], keep it, but do
not claim it is measured.

```bash
if grep -qiE "(it|this|that) ?(is|was|'s)? ?(not|n'?t) (just |only |merely |about )?[a-z' ]{2,40}[.,] +(it'?s|it was|that'?s|it is)" "$DRAFT" \
   || grep -qiE "not (only|merely|just) [a-z' ]{2,40}(,| ) ?but( also)? " "$DRAFT"; then
  echo "SOFT  structure: antithesis pivot (\"not X, it's Y\" / \"not only X but also Y\")"; soft=$((soft+1))
fi
```

Tested — and the first draft of this regex failed, which is why the results are listed rather than
asserted:

| Input | Fires? | Matched text |
|---|---|---|
| `It's not just about the gear, it's about the vision.` (`slop.md`) | yes | `It's not just about the gear, it's` |
| `This wasn't just a concert. It was a moment.` | yes | `This wasn't just a concert. It was` |
| `It is not only loud but also relentless.` | yes | second alternation |
| `evals/fixtures/good.md` | no | — |
| 7 real captions | no | — |
| `I brought one body, one lens, and a spare battery.` | no | — |

The published `it'?s not …` form in the current script and my own first rewrite both **missed
`wasn't just X. It was Y`**, because they required a literal `not` after the pronoun rather than
allowing the contracted `n't`. The version above allows `is|was|'s` + `not|n't`, which is what
makes the second row pass.

**FP risk: moderate, and demonstrated.** On a hand-written human-antithesis file it fires twice:

```
it wasn't the loudest show, it was the one I remember
this isn't a review, it's just what I saw from the pit
```

Both are things this creator could plausibly write, and both are genuinely the construction.
That is the argument for keeping it **SOFT** — one hit is a prompt to look, which is the existing
contract. Do **not** promote it to HARD.

### 4.3 Em-dash — **change the threshold and fix the hard rule**

Per §1.7 the em-dash is a real population-level signal in formal prose and explicitly *not* a
per-document detector. The current `> 3` threshold is calibrated for essays. In a 15-word caption,
**one** em-dash is already anomalous — and for *this* creator it is a hard rule they stated.

```bash
dashes=$(grep -o '—' "$DRAFT" | wc -l | tr -d ' ')
words=$(wc -w < "$DRAFT" | tr -d ' ')
if (( words < 80 )); then
  (( dashes >= 1 )) && { echo "SOFT  structure: em-dash in a short caption"; soft=$((soft+1)); }
else
  (( dashes > 3 )) && { echo "SOFT  structure: $dashes em-dashes"; soft=$((soft+1)); }
fi
```

**FP risk: low for this creator, high in general.** The 80-word gate is what keeps it honest — the
threshold must scale with length or it punishes anyone who writes long-form with dashes. The
SlopDetector caveat is the right one to carry: prolific dash-users trip any dash threshold, and
this is not forensic on its own.

#### 4.3.1 The hard-rule fix

`data/voice.md` lists `em dash (—)`; the HARD stage greps for that literal string, so a real
em-dash never matches (§2.1, Defect 2). Two options, in preference order:

1. **Script-side (no data change):** before the `grep -qiF`, if the entry contains a parenthetical,
   match on the parenthetical's contents instead of the whole label.

   **Do not use the obvious `[[ =~ ]]` version — it is a trap, and I walked into it.** This:
   ```bash
   if [[ "$phrase" =~ \(([^\)]+)\)$ ]]; then lit="${BASH_REMATCH[1]}"; fi
   ```
   matches with an **empty capture** on every entry that has no parentheses, leaving `lit=""`, and
   `grep -qiF -- ""` matches any file. Tested: it turned all 8 deny-list entries into HARD hits on
   every input, including `evals/fixtures/good.md`. It converts the gate into an unconditional
   blocker.

   The parameter-expansion version is correct and has no regex to get wrong:
   ```bash
   # inside the deny-list loop, replacing the bare grep
   lit="$phrase"
   case "$phrase" in
     *\(*\)) inner="${phrase##*\(}"; inner="${inner%\)}"
             [[ -n "$inner" ]] && lit="$inner" ;;
   esac
   [[ -z "$lit" ]] && continue
   if grep -qiF -- "$lit" "$DRAFT"; then ...; fi
   ```
   Verified 2026-08-21 against the real `data/voice.md` deny-list:

   | Input | hard hits | Correct? |
   |---|---|---|
   | `the lights went down — and then it started` | 1 — `em dash (—)` matched as `—` | ✅ the defect is fixed |
   | 7 real captions from `voice.md` | 0 | ✅ no regression |
   | `evals/fixtures/good.md` | 0 | ✅ |
   | `evals/fixtures/slop.md` | 0 | ✅ |
   | `what an amazing show, unforgettable night` | 2 | ✅ existing behaviour preserved |

   **FP risk: low, but real** — any deny-list entry ending in a parenthetical gets narrowed to the
   parenthetical. Worth a comment line in `voice.md`'s template explaining the convention.
2. **Data-side:** ask `ff-init` to write the literal character as its own bullet (`—`) rather than
   a description. Cleaner, but rewrites a file the creator authored, so it is the second choice.

This is a `ff-critique` / `slop-check.sh` bug regardless of what happens to `ai-tells.txt`.

### 4.4 Sentence-initial discourse markers — **implement, count-based**

Evidence: `however` r=1.06 and `moreover` r=0.97 are flat *overall* — so the word is not the tell.
What is elevated is `additionally` (2.13×), `ultimately` (1.67×), and, per Russell et al., the
*formulaic connective habit* itself. Counting them at sentence-start separates "a human used
however once" from "a model is stringing paragraphs together".

```bash
markers=$(grep -oiE '(^|[.!?][[:space:]]+)(moreover|furthermore|additionally|however|ultimately|overall|in conclusion|in essence|notably|importantly|that said)\b' "$DRAFT" | wc -l | tr -d ' ')
if (( markers >= 2 )); then
  echo "SOFT  structure: $markers sentence-initial discourse markers"; soft=$((soft+1))
fi
```

Tested: **2** hits on the adversarial slop caption (`Moreover`, `Furthermore`), **0** on all seven
real captions, **0** on `good.md`, **0** on `evals/fixtures/slop.md` — note that last one: the
existing slop fixture contains no discourse markers, so this check adds coverage the fixture does
not currently exercise. If it ships, `evals/fixtures/slop.md` should gain a line that trips it.

**FP risk: low at threshold 2, high at threshold 1.** Keep the `>= 2`. Note the leading-`(^|[.!?] )`
group is what prevents firing on "however" mid-sentence, which is normal English.

### 4.5 "Conclusion restates the opening" — **implement, long-form only**

`slop-patterns.md` already names this pattern with no mechanical check. It is measurable as
content-word overlap between the first and last non-empty lines, in pure awk:

```bash
echoed=$(awk '{gsub(/[^A-Za-z \047]/," "); if($0 ~ /[A-Za-z]/){ if(!first) first=tolower($0); last=tolower($0)} }
END{ n=split(first,a," "); m=split(last,b," ");
 stop=" the a an and or but is was it its to of in on for with that this you i my me we not just about more than from ";
 for(i=1;i<=n;i++) if(length(a[i])>3 && index(stop," " a[i] " ")==0) A[a[i]]=1;
 for(j=1;j<=m;j++) if(length(b[j])>3 && index(stop," " b[j] " ")==0 && (b[j] in A)) k++;
 print k+0 }' "$DRAFT")
lines=$(grep -c '[A-Za-z]' "$DRAFT")
if (( lines >= 4 && echoed >= 3 )); then
  echo "SOFT  structure: closing line restates the opening ($echoed shared content words)"; soft=$((soft+1))
fi
```

Tested: returns **5** on a purpose-built draft whose last line is `Ultimately, concert photography
rewards patience more than gear.` against an opener of `Concert photography rewards patience more
than gear.` Returns **0** on the real captions and on `good.md`.

**FP risk: moderate, and length-gated for that reason.** A short caption whose payoff *is* a
deliberate callback would trip it — that is a legitimate rhetorical move (`hooks.md` territory).
The `lines >= 4` gate keeps it off captions entirely; it is for YouTube descriptions and scripts.
The stop-list is crude and English-only; that is acceptable for a SOFT signal.

### 4.6 Anaphora / parallel openers — **implement**

Not in the current doctrine. "Some nights X. Some nights Y. Some nights Z." is the caption-length
cousin of the tricolon and is exactly the shape a model reaches for.

```bash
anaph=$(awk '{n=split(tolower($0),s,/[.!?\n]+ */); for(i=1;i<=n;i++){split(s[i],w," "); if(w[1]!="")c[w[1]]++}}
 END{max=0; for(k in c) if(c[k]>max) max=c[k]; print max}' "$DRAFT")
if (( anaph >= 3 )); then
  echo "SOFT  structure: $anaph sentences opening with the same word"; soft=$((soft+1))
fi
```

Tested: returns **3** on `Some nights stay with you. Some nights change you. Some nights you never
forget.` (fires at threshold 3); returns **2** across the seven real captions, **1** on `good.md`,
**2** on `evals/fixtures/slop.md` — all below threshold, so no false positive, but the margin on the
real captions is one. That thin margin is the reason for the per-draft rule below, and the reason
not to lower the threshold to 2.

**FP risk: moderate.** Deliberate anaphora is a real human device and this creator's Kodaline
register uses second-person openers repeatedly across *separate* captions — the check must run
per-draft, never across a batch file. Threshold 3, never 2.

### 4.7 Checks that are NOT worth implementing

| Candidate | Why not |
|---|---|
| **Burstiness / sentence-length CV** | §4.0. Needs ≥8 sentences; captions give 2–3, and this creator omits terminal punctuation entirely, so the segmenter fails before the statistic does. Viable only for long-form drafts, and even there it is a *P2* metric — see the warning below |
| **Perplexity** | Requires a language model. Not computable in bash by any honest route. Also the wrong target: perplexity is the detector's metric, and §6 is the reason not to chase it |
| **Hedging density** | Measured 0 occurrences on both the slop fixture and the real captions. Hedging is a long-form academic tell; captions are too short for the denominator to mean anything |

## 5. Human perception, and what it implies for the gate

This is the section the brief called most important, and the evidence here is unusually good.

### 5.1 Ordinary readers cannot detect AI text, and the cues they report are wrong

> **Jakesch, Hancock & Naaman**, *Human heuristics for AI-generated language are flawed*,
> ***PNAS*** 120(11), 2023 (peer-reviewed); [10.1073/pnas.2208839120](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2208839120).
> **Six experiments, 4,600 participants**, self-presentations in professional, hospitality and
> dating contexts.

- Detection accuracy was **at chance** in the three main experiments and close to chance across all
  three contexts.
- **Monetary incentives for accuracy and per-trial feedback did not improve it.**
- A computational analysis identified the heuristics people actually apply: **first-person
  pronouns, contractions, and references to family**. People read these as human.
- Those heuristics are **predictable and manipulable**: the authors generated AI text that
  exploited them and produced language rated **"more human than human."**

**Implication for the gate.** The features a reader consciously names as "human" are cheap to fake
and therefore worthless as a quality target. A gate that optimizes for contractions and first-person
pronouns is optimizing for a manipulable proxy. The plugin already has the right target —
`slop-patterns.md`'s "could someone who was not there have written this?" — and this study is the
reason to keep it as the *primary* test with the word list explicitly subordinate.

### 5.2 Heavy LLM users detect it near-perfectly, and their cues are the real spec

> **Russell, Karpinska & Iyyer**, *People who frequently use ChatGPT for writing tasks are accurate
> and robust detectors of AI-generated text*, ***ACL 2025***, main conference (peer-reviewed);
> [aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.267](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.267/);
> [arXiv 2501.15654](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.15654). **300 non-fiction English articles**,
> annotators wrote paragraph-length justifications for every judgement; models included GPT-4o,
> Claude, o1.

- **Majority vote of five frequent-LLM-user annotators misclassified 1 of 300 articles.** They beat
  most commercial and open-source detectors — **including on paraphrased and "humanized" text.**
- No training or feedback was given. Frequency of personal LLM use was the differentiator.
- Their **14 cue categories, with the share of explanations citing each** (Table 4):

| Rank | Cue | % | What they said |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Vocabulary** | 53.1% | Repetitive, unnatural wording. Named examples: *vibrant, crucial, significantly, delve, testament, adventurous, chunky, musky* |
| 2 | **Sentence structure** | 35.9% | Predictable patterns — "not only … but also", consistently listing exactly three items; humans vary sentence length more |
| 3 | **Grammar & punctuation** | 24.8% | AI is "usually grammatically perfect" and **avoids dashes, ellipses and brackets** that humans use; human writing has minor errors |
| 4 | **Originality** | 23.7% | "Straightforward, safe, and lacking in surprises" |
| 5 | Quotes | 22.3% | AI quotes sound overly formal and mirror the main text's style |
| 6 | Clarity | 19.5% | Over-explains; "telling" not "showing" |
| 7 | Formatting | 15.0% | Overly consistent: uniform paragraph lengths, bolded lists |
| 8 | **Conclusions** | 13.1% | "Repetitive and overly optimistic summaries" with a "flowery tone"; **humans end more abruptly** |
| 9 | Formality | 12.3% | Rarely uses filler words, contractions, slang |
| 10 | Names & titles | 11.7% | Generic recurring names, uniform titles |
| 11 | **Tone** | 9.3% | "Consistently neutral or positive", lacking emotional variety; inherent **positivity bias** |
| 12 | Introductions | 7.3% | Generic, scenic-detail openers rather than a hook |
| 13 | Factuality | 7.2% | Hallucinations delivered with "customary AI confidence" |
| 14 | Topics | 3.1% | **Avoids darker or more mature material**, focuses on hopeful elements |

Note the direct contradiction with §5.1: naive readers use contractions as a *human* signal; expert
detectors use their absence as an *AI* signal. Same feature, opposite reliability. The difference is
that experts use it as one of fourteen weighted cues, not as a single heuristic.

**Implication for the gate, and it is the biggest single design finding in this file:**
**vocabulary is 53.1% of what expert humans actually cite.** The deny-list is not a minor
"floor" — it is over half the perceptual signal. That justifies making `ai-tells.txt` *good*
rather than deprecating it, which is what §2 and §3 are for. But rows 4, 8, 11, and 14 —
originality, flowery optimistic conclusions, flat positive tone, avoidance of darker material —
are semantic and can only be caught by `ff-critique`'s judgement pass. The two-stage architecture
is correct; this table is a better spec for stage two than the current prose.

**Row 14 and row 11 deserve special weight for this creator.** The voice profile's strongest
material is a breakup register — *"you were the one who ruined it"*, *"you muted it instead of
blocking it"*. Expert detectors say models avoid exactly that darkness and default to hopeful
positivity. The creator's best captions are already on the far side of the strongest perceptual
cue. That should be stated as doctrine, not left implicit.

### 5.3 Is there an "uncanny valley" for over-polished writing?

**Not as a named, measured effect.** What exists is adjacent and one-directional: expert detectors
cite grammatical perfection and uniform polish as AI cues (§5.2 rows 3 and 7), and *Originality*
at 23.7% describes safe, surprise-free prose. That is consistent with an uncanny-valley story but
nobody located has measured a **non-monotonic** relationship — polish improving perceived quality
up to a point and then reversing. Anyone writing "there is an uncanny valley for polished prose"
into the doctrine would be over-claiming. See §9.

The nearest real finding is about *attribution*, not polish:

> **Haverals & Martin**, *Everyone prefers human writers, including AI*, [arXiv 2510.08831](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08831),
> October 2025 — **preprint, not peer-reviewed.** Study 1: **556 human participants** and 13 AI
> models judging passages from Queneau's *Exercises in Style* vs GPT-4 imitations, under blind,
> correctly-labelled, and **falsely-labelled** conditions.
> Humans preferred human-attributed writing by **+13.7 pp**; AI judges by **+34.3 pp** (2.5× the
> human bias). Identical text received opposing evaluations depending on the label alone.

### 5.4 The disclosure and suspicion penalty — the strongest evidence in this section

| Study | Status | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Schilke & Reimann**, *The transparency dilemma: How AI disclosure erodes trust*, ***Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes***, 2025; [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0749597825000172) | **Peer-reviewed** | **13 experiments** across tasks (analytics to artistry) and actors (supervisors, subordinates, professors, analysts, **creatives**, funds) | Actors who **disclose** AI use are trusted **less** than those who do not. Mediated by reduced perceived **legitimacy**. Crucially: **framing the disclosure differently, prior knowledge of the AI use, and making disclosure mandatory vs. voluntary all fail to prevent the erosion** |
| **Reif, Larrick & Soll**, *Evidence of a social evaluation penalty for using AI*, ***PNAS*** 122(19), 2025; [10.1073/pnas.2426766122](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2426766122) | **Peer-reviewed** | **4 preregistered experiments, 4,439 participants** | People who use AI at work both **anticipate and receive** lower ratings of competence and motivation. Evaluators who themselves use AI less than weekly rate AI-users as **lazy**, lowering perceived task fit. The penalty **shrinks when AI is obviously appropriate for the task** |

**Implication for the gate — this is the whole argument for its existence.** The cost of reading
as AI-written is not a detector flag, it is a **legitimacy and competence penalty applied by the
reader**, and Schilke & Reimann show it cannot be talked away by framing. For a creator whose
entire product is "I was there and I noticed this", a reader who suspects AI has withdrawn the one
attribution the content depends on. That reframes the gate: it is not a style filter, it is
**protection of the authorship claim.**

Two honest limits: both studies test *disclosed or known* AI use, not *suspected* AI use from
stylistic cues. The bridge from "reader suspects" to "reader penalizes" is an inference, and should
be labelled as one wherever it lands in the doctrine. And both are Western workplace/consumer
populations.

### 5.5 The finding that matters most for a Philippines-based creator

> **Agarwal, Naaman & Vashistha**, *AI Suggestions Homogenize Writing Toward Western Styles and
> Diminish Cultural Nuances*, **CHI 2025** (peer-reviewed);
> [10.1145/3706598.3713564](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3706598.3713564);
> [arXiv 2409.11360](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.11360). **Cross-cultural controlled experiment,
> 118 participants** from India and the US, culturally grounded writing tasks with and without AI
> suggestions.

- AI suggestions delivered **greater efficiency gains for American participants** than Indian ones.
- Indian participants **shifted toward Western writing styles** when using AI — changing not only
  what was written but **how**.
- The authors describe the outcome as cultural stereotyping and language homogenization.

**Implication.** The risk to this creator from using an AI writing assistant is not only "sounds
like ChatGPT" — it is "sounds like a Western writer." India is not the Philippines and the study
does not generalize automatically, but it is the only controlled experiment located on this exact
mechanism, and the direction is the one that matters. It argues for the plugin weighting
`data/voice.md` — the creator's actual recorded sentences — above any universal list, which is
already the stated architecture. This study is the evidence for that decision.

### 5.6 Six design rules the perception evidence supports

1. **Keep the word list, and make it good.** 53.1% of expert human cues are vocabulary (§5.2).
2. **Never treat a word hit as proof.** Ordinary readers judge at chance (§5.1); the creator's own
   proven caption trips the current list (§2.1).
3. **Voice profile outranks the universal list, always.** §5.5, plus the existing `voice.md` note.
4. **Target the reader's feeling, never the detector's score.** §6.
5. **Protect the darkness.** Expert detectors' rarest-cited cue (topics, 3.1%) and their tone cue
   (9.3%) both say models default to hopeful positivity. This creator's best work is not hopeful.
   A gate that sands that down is doing the damage it exists to prevent.
6. **Let the ending be abrupt.** Row 8: humans end more abruptly; AI writes flowery optimistic
   summaries. "Sing it back." is the right shape.

## 6. Detector reliability and the ESL problem

> **Liang, Yuksekgonul, Mao, Wu & Zou**, *GPT detectors are biased against non-native English
> writers*, ***Patterns*** 4(7):100779, 2023 (peer-reviewed, Cell Press);
> [journal](https://www.cell.com/patterns/fulltext/S2666-3899(23)00130-7);
> [arXiv 2304.02819](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819).
> **Seven widely-used commercial GPT detectors**, two human-written corpora: US eighth-grade
> student essays (native) and **TOEFL essays written by non-native English speakers**.

- **More than half** of the non-native-authored TOEFL essays were misclassified as AI-generated.
- **One detector flagged ~98%** of them.
- The same detectors were **near-perfect** on the native-speaker eighth-grade essays.
- Mechanism: detectors key on **low lexical/syntactic variety**, so they penalize "writers with
  constrained linguistic expressions."
- Simple prompting both **mitigates the bias and defeats the detectors**, which is the clearest
  possible statement that detector scores are not a quality signal.

**This settles the design question the brief raised.** The creator writes English as a second
language. A gate that optimized toward "not tripping detectors" would be optimizing toward the
exact axis on which those detectors already misjudge ESL writers by more than 50%, and would push
the creator toward more elaborate, more varied, less characteristic English — which is also the
direction §5.5 warns about. It would produce worse captions *and* a false sense of safety.

**Recommended doctrine line:** *frame-first does not check whether a draft would trip an AI
detector, and will not. Published detectors misclassify over half of non-native-English human
writing as AI-generated (Liang et al., Patterns 2023). The only target is whether a human reader
believes a person was there.*

One further note for honesty: Russell et al. (§5.2) found expert *humans* beat most commercial
detectors, including under paraphrase attack. So the detectors are not merely biased — on this
task they are also not state of the art.

## 7. De-slopping techniques and how thin the evidence is

**Being direct as the brief asked: this is the weakest section, and it is mostly craft.** No
randomized study was located that measures "does editing pass X reduce a human reader's AI
impression of short-form captions." What exists:

| Technique | Evidence | Honest grade |
|---|---|---|
| **Injecting specific witness detail** | Indirect but real. Concrete language raises *perceived truth* — already documented in `research/2026-08-20-sharing-psychology.md` §1.5 and carried into `relatability.md`. Separately, expert detectors cite Originality (23.7%) and "telling not showing" (Clarity, 19.5%) as AI cues. **Nothing measures the intervention directly** | **Best-supported, and still indirect.** Two independent lines converge on it, which is more than anything else here has |
| **Removing over-represented vocabulary** | §1 gives the word list, §5.2 gives 53.1% cue weight. But **no study measures that removing the words changes reader judgement** | Strong premises, unmeasured conclusion |
| **Prompting the model not to do it** | Liang et al. (Patterns 2023) show simple prompting changes detector-relevant style, and one located study piloted a prompting intervention against persona distortion. Neither measures the ChatGPT *feeling* | Weak. Plausible, unmeasured for this purpose |
| **Deliberately varying rhythm** | Supported only as a *description* of human writing (Russell et al.: "humans vary sentence length more"). **Doing it on purpose is P2 thinking** and §4.7 argues against building it | Weak, and possibly counterproductive |
| **Injecting deliberate errors / lowercase / typos** | Expert detectors cite "minor errors" as a human cue (24.8%). But this is precisely the manipulable-heuristic trap of §5.1 — and for an ESL writer, deliberately degrading grammar is advice with a real cost | **Reject.** Note that this creator's lowercase register is a *voice* choice recorded in `voice.md`, not an error-injection tactic, and the distinction matters |
| **Writing it yourself first, then editing** | No evidence located. §5.5 gives a reason to prefer it (AI suggestions homogenize non-Western writers), but that is an argument, not a measurement | Craft advice with a supporting mechanism |

**What to say in the doctrine:** the only de-slopping move with converging support is *add the
detail that required being there, and cut the words the corpus studies show models over-produce*.
Everything else — rhythm variation, error injection, humanizing prompts — is craft, tactic, or
detector-chasing, and should be labelled that way rather than presented as technique.

### Tells of AI prose — what is measured, and what is taste

Two different problems live under "this sounds like AI", and conflating them produces bad edits:

- **The feeling.** A reader decides nobody was there and nobody chose these words. This is the
  target.
- **Detector scores.** Not the target, and actively harmful to chase — published detectors
  misclassify **more than half** of non-native-English human writing as AI-generated, and one
  flagged 98% of it (Liang et al., *Patterns* 2023, seven commercial detectors, TOEFL corpus).
  frame-first does not optimize for detector output and never will.

**What expert readers actually notice.** Annotators who use LLMs daily identify AI text almost
perfectly — five of them voting together misclassified 1 article in 300, beating commercial
detectors even on "humanized" text (Russell et al., ACL 2025, 300 articles, written justifications
for every call). The share of their explanations citing each cue, top eight:

| Cue | Share | In practice |
|---|---|---|
| Vocabulary | 53.1% | Words models over-produce. See `ai-tells.txt` — over half the signal |
| Sentence structure | 35.9% | "not only … but also"; runs of exactly three items; uniform sentence lengths |
| Grammar & punctuation | 24.8% | Grammatically perfect; avoids the dashes, ellipses and brackets people actually type |
| Originality | 23.7% | Safe. No surprises. Nothing that could only have occurred to one person |
| Clarity | 19.5% | Over-explains. Tells instead of shows |
| Conclusions | 13.1% | Flowery, optimistic summary. **Humans end more abruptly** |
| Tone | 9.3% | Uniform neutral-positive. No emotional variation |
| Topics | 3.1% | Avoids darker or harder material; steers toward hopeful |

**The last two rows are the ones that matter most here.** A model defaults to hopeful and even.
"you were the one who ruined it" and "you muted it instead of blocking it" are on the far side of
that default — the darkness *is* the human signal, and a polish pass that softens it removes the
strongest thing in the caption. Same for endings: stop when the thought stops. "Sing it back."

**Which words are actually over-represented — and which are folklore.** Two corpus studies measure
this. Kobak et al. (*Science Advances* 2025) analysed **>15M PubMed abstracts, 2010–2024** and
identified 900 excess words, of which 407 are pure style words. Liang et al. (ICML 2024) analysed
**~49,000 conference peer reviews**. Words confirmed elevated by both: **meticulous, intricate,
commendable**. Words with the largest measured shifts: **delves** (≈48× more frequent in 2024 than
2022), **underscores** (≈14×), **showcasing** (≈14×), **delve** (≈10×), **intricate** (≈7×).

Words the same data **refutes** — measured flat or *declining* since 2022, and therefore removed
from the deny-list: *breathtaking* (0.85×), *stunning* (0.94×), *speechless* (0.81×), *magic*
(0.96×), *myriad* (0.99×), *plethora* (0.83×), *truly* (0.93×), *deep dive* (1.1–1.3×). These are
ordinary human words in a concert caption. Flagging them is how a gate loses trust. **"hits
different" and "rent free" are internet-native slang that predates ChatGPT entirely** — and "hits
different" appears in this creator's own best-performing Instagram caption.

**The em-dash.** The discourse is mostly folklore but the effect is real: em-dash prevalence in
medRxiv Discussion sections rose from **4.23% to 11.58%** pre- vs post-ChatGPT across **69,632
preprints** (pre-registered, OSF HFT8C), and more than doubled across 10,000 ecology abstracts,
with no other punctuation mark close. The authors' own caveat is the important part: it is a
**population-level indicator, not a per-document detector**. One em-dash in a 15-word caption is
still worth looking at — this creator has it on their own deny-list — but a dash-heavy long-form
writer is not thereby a machine.

**Why the over-used words are over-used.** They are the words human raters *preferred* during
alignment training (Juzek & Ward, COLING 2025 and BIAS 2025). Not a coding bug — a taste
transplant. That is why the vocabulary reads as impressive and empty at the same time, and why the
list will drift as models retrain. `ai-tells.txt` carries a review date for that reason.

**A caution specific to writing English as a second language.** In a controlled experiment with 118
participants, AI writing suggestions moved non-Western writers toward Western style — changing not
just what was written but how (Agarwal, Naaman & Vashistha, CHI 2025). The defence is
`data/voice.md`: real recorded sentences the creator actually wrote outrank every universal list in
this plugin, including this one.

**What is at stake if a reader suspects AI.** Not reach. Trust. Across **13 experiments**, actors
who disclosed AI use were trusted less, mediated by reduced perceived legitimacy — and reframing
the disclosure did not help (Schilke & Reimann, *OBHDP* 2025). Across **4 preregistered experiments
with 4,439 participants**, AI users were rated lower on competence and motivation and read as lazy
(Reif, Larrick & Soll, *PNAS* 2025). Both studies test *known* AI use rather than *suspected* AI
use, so the bridge is an inference — but for a creator whose whole claim is "I was there and I
noticed this", a reader who stops believing the authorship has stopped buying the product.
