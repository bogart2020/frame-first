# Sharing psychology research — 2026-08-20

Research pass only. No skill or reference file was changed. Prompted by the creator's observation
that "relatable captions are so engaging," and by the fact that
[`references/ranking-signals.md`](../references/ranking-signals.md) records **reshare** as
Instagram's own top-ranked Reels prediction — which makes "what causes a share" the highest-value
open question in the plugin, and the one
[`2026-08-20-diagnostics.md` §6 row 1](2026-08-20-diagnostics.md) flags as having zero sourcing.

This file does not fix that gap. **No platform publishes anything about what causes a reshare, and
no peer-reviewed study measures shares of short-form vertical video.** What it does is replace an
unsourced hunch with a labelled body of academic evidence, and mark exactly where that evidence
stops transferring.

Every paper below was located through PubMed/E-utilities, Crossref, or OpenAlex and is named with
journal, year, N, and method. Where only an abstract was reachable, that is stated in the row.

Conventions, extending [`2026-08-20-hardening.md`](2026-08-20-hardening.md) and
[`2026-08-20-diagnostics.md`](2026-08-20-diagnostics.md):

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Established** | Multiple independent studies, or one large preregistered study plus a replication. Direction is safe to act on. |
| **Suggestive** | Real peer-reviewed finding, but single study, small N, old medium, or unreplicated. Act on it as a bet, label it as one. |
| **Contested** | Competent studies point opposite ways. Naming the disagreement *is* the finding. |
| **Refuted** | A well-powered replication or re-analysis failed to find it. Do not use, even softened. |
| **Folklore** | Widely repeated, no traceable study with a stated method. Refuse. |
| **Full text** / **Abstract only** | Whether the claim was read from the paper or from its abstract. |

**Medium warning, applied throughout.** The canonical virality literature measures *emailing a
newspaper article* (2008), *sharing a link on Facebook/Twitter*, or *sharing a video ad*. The
creator's medium is a short vertical video with on-screen caption text, shared via DM. Nothing
below was measured on that. Rows are graded on how far the transfer is.

## 1. What is well established

### 1.1 Sharing is self-presentation and social bonding — and this now has a preregistered cross-cultural replication

This is the single strongest result in the whole literature, and it is the one the plugin already
bets on without a citation.

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Berger (2014), *Journal of Consumer Psychology* 24(4):586–607, [10.1016/j.jcps.2014.05.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.05.002). **Full text of abstract; review article** | Theoretical review | Word of mouth is goal-driven and serves five functions: **impression management, emotion regulation, information acquisition, social bonding, persuasion**. Verbatim: these motivations "are predominantly self‐ (rather than other) serving and drive what people talk about even without their awareness" |
| Berger (2025), *Annual Review of Psychology*, [10.1146/annurev-psych-013024-031524](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-013024-031524). **Abstract only** | Updated review | Same author's current synthesis. Adds that audience, modality, channel, and device moderate what gets shared — i.e. the *same content* travels differently by channel |
| Scholz, Baek, O'Donnell, Kim, Cappella & Falk (2017), *PNAS* 114(11):2881–2886, [10.1073/pnas.1615259114](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1615259114). **Full abstract** | Two fMRI studies, **n = 41 and n = 39**, reading 80 NYT articles; linked to **117,611 logged real internet shares** of those articles | Activity in self-related and social-cognition regions predicted population sharing **indirectly, through the brain's value system**. Neural activity predicted sharing "over and above the variance explained by article characteristics and commonly used self-report measures of sharing intentions" |
| Chan, Scholz, Cosme, Martin, Benitez, Resnick, Carreras-Tartak, Cooper, Paul & Falk (2023), *PNAS* 120(44):e2313175120, [10.1073/pnas.2313175120](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2313175120). **Full abstract** | **Preregistered** neuroimaging, US **and** Netherlands | Replicates Scholz et al. Prediction models *trained on the 2017 data* generalized to new data and explained more variance in population sharing than self-report alone. Neural signals predicted sharing cross-culturally more reliably than self-reports |
| Tamir & Mitchell (2012), *PNAS* 109(21):8038–43, [10.1073/pnas.1202129109](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1202129109). **Full abstract** | Five studies, fMRI + behavioural | Self-disclosure activates the mesolimbic dopamine system (nucleus accumbens, VTA). **People will forgo money for the opportunity to disclose about themselves.** Two further studies separate the value of self-referential thought from the value of simply sharing information with someone |

**Verdict: Established.** Two of these are preregistered or cross-culturally replicated, and one
links brain data to six figures of real logged shares rather than to intentions.

> **Caption implication.** A caption's job at the Sharing stage is not to describe the footage. It
> is to hand the sender a sentence about *themselves* that they can pass to a specific person.
> Scholz/Chan say the two inputs that get integrated into share-value are **self-relevance** and
> **social relevance** — which is precisely the send test in
> [`references/arrrsr.md`](../references/arrrsr.md), and it can now be cited rather than asserted.
> The "who forwards this, to whom specifically, and what does sending it say about the sender?"
> test survives contact with the literature intact. Keep it verbatim.

### 1.2 Self-referential encoding is a real and old memory effect — this is the mechanism under "relatable"

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Symons & Johnson (1997), *Psychological Bulletin* 121(3):371–94, [10.1037/0033-2909.121.3.371](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.121.3.371). **Full abstract** | **Meta-analysis** | Self-referent encoding yields superior memory relative to **both** semantic and other-referent encoding. Mechanism: the self is a well-developed, often-used construct that promotes elaboration and organization of encoded material |
| Sweatman, He, Lawrence & Chai (2025), *Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience* 74:101581, [10.1016/j.dcn.2025.101581](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dcn.2025.101581). **Full abstract** | fMRI, **N = 83**, ages 7–25 | "Self-referential compared to semantic encoding resulted in better recollection across all ages" — the effect holds from childhood through adulthood |
| He, Sweatman, Thomson, Gracia-Tabuenca, Puts & Chai (2025), *Journal of Neuroscience* 45(25):e2343242025, [10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2343-24.2025](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2343-24.2025). **Full abstract** | MRS + fMRI, **N = 54** | Self-related condition led to better subsequent memory and greater precuneus/PCC activation than the semantic condition |

**Verdict: Established** for memory. A 1997 meta-analysis plus independent 2025 replications with
different methods.

**What it does NOT establish:** that self-referential framing increases *sharing*. That hop is
mine, and it is labelled as a hop everywhere it appears below.

> **Caption implication, and the one that answers the creator's actual observation.** "Relatable"
> is not a vibe — it is a *processing instruction*. A caption phrased so the viewer has to check
> it against their own experience is encoded self-referentially; a caption phrased as description
> is encoded semantically. That is a mechanical difference with 25+ years of memory evidence
> behind it.
>
> | Semantic encoding (describes the world) | Self-referential encoding (forces a check against the self) |
> |---|---|
> | "Everyone's phone camera struggles in low light." | "You've got one photo from that night and you can't tell which one is your friend." |
> | "Parking at these things is a nightmare." | "You know the exact moment you decided to just take the ticket." |
>
> The right column is not more emotional. It is the same claim, aimed so the reader has to run it
> against a memory to parse it.

### 1.3 Discrete emotions beat both valence and arousal — which is a correction, not a confirmation

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Paletz, Johns, Murauskaite, Golonka, Pandža, Rytting, Buntain & Ellis (2023), *Science Advances* 9(39), [10.1126/sciadv.ade9231](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.ade9231). **Full abstract** | **>4,000 multimedia posts** from Polish and Lithuanian Facebook, annotated by teams for **>20 emotions**; models compared head to head | Modelling **discrete emotions independently was superior** to models using valence, activation/arousal, or emotion clusters. Emotions associated with sharing included "both positive and negative and relatively lower and higher activation/arousal emotions (e.g., **amusement, cute/kama muta, anger, and sadness**)" — controlling for follower count, time up, topic, and Facebook angry reactions |

This is the only study I found that **directly pitted the competing theories against each other**
on the same dataset. Arousal lost. Valence lost. Discrete emotions won.

**Verdict: Established** that the discrete-emotion framing is the right one. `arrrsr.md`'s line
"discrete emotions predict sharing; positivity alone does not" is **correct and now citable** —
this study is its evidence.

**But note what it did to sadness.** Sadness was among the emotions *positively* associated with
sharing. That is the opposite of Berger & Milkman (see §2.1 and §4.2).

### 1.4 Clickbait and manipulative framing reduce shares — with a named mechanism

This is the single most useful result in the file for the plugin's anti-slop premise, because it
supplies the *mechanism* the doctrine currently asserts on taste.

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Mukherjee, Dutta & De Bruyn (2022), *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* 50(3):482–502, [10.1007/s11747-021-00830-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-021-00830-x). **Full abstract** | Controlled experiment **plus 19,386 articles from 27 leading online publishers**, with Twitter share data, sentiment analysis, topic modelling and controls for endogenous selection | Verbatim: "clickbait tactics may be perceived by some readers as a **manipulative attempt**, leading to **source derogation** where the publisher may be perceived as less competent and trustworthy… we confirm that, on average, **clickbait articles elicit far fewer shares than non-clickbait articles**. Our results are stable, with **large effect sizes** even after controlling for endogenous selection." Separately: "**high-emotional headlines are shared more**" |

**Verdict: Established.** Experiment plus a five-figure field sample plus effect-size stability.

> **Caption implication, and it is a sharper distinction than the plugin currently draws.** The
> study separates two things the plugin's `slop-patterns.md` tends to blur:
>
> - **Emotional intensity → more shares.** Not the problem.
> - **Perceived manipulative intent → source derogation → fewer shares.** The problem.
>
> So the failure mode is not "too emotional." It is "reads as engineered to make me react." An
> unclosed curiosity gap, "tag someone who needs to see this," and "which would you pick? 😍"
> all fail on the *same* mechanism — the reader infers a tactic and downgrades the source. That
> also independently converges with Meta's own demotion policy in `ranking-signals.md`, from a
> completely different direction.

### 1.5 Concrete language raises perceived truth — the real support for "witness detail"

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Hansen & Wänke (2010), *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* 36(11):1576–88, [10.1177/0146167210386238](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167210386238). **Full abstract** | Four experiments | "Statements of the **very same content** were judged as **more probably true** when they were written in concrete language than when they were written in abstract language." Mediated by **greater perceived vividness** of concrete statements. Effect amplified under a concrete mindset (Exp. 3) or spatial proximity (Exp. 4) |
| van Laer, de Ruyter, Visconti & Wetzels (2014), *Journal of Consumer Research* 40(5):797–817, [10.1086/673383](https://doi.org/10.1086/673383). **Full text of preprint** | **Meta-analysis: 132 effect sizes from 76 published and unpublished articles** | Of the antecedents of narrative transportation **under the storyteller's control**: imaginable plot **ρ = .29**, verisimilitude **ρ = .27**, identifiable characters **ρ = .20** — all small but significant. Verisimilitude is defined not as factual accuracy but as "**the likelihood that story events may actually happen**"; "the central focus of verisimilitude is on **believability**" |
| Green & Brock (2000), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 79(5):701–721, [10.1037/0022-3514.79.5.701](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.5.701). **Abstract + secondary N confirmation** | Four experiments, **N = 97, 69, 274, 258**; Exp. 4 manipulated transportation directly via processing instructions | Transportation increased story-consistent beliefs and favourable protagonist evaluations; highly transported readers found **fewer false notes** in a story |

**Verdict: Established** that concreteness raises believability, and that believability/imaginability
are the two levers a storyteller actually holds.

**Honest complication, and it matters.** See §2.5 — the classic *vividness effect* on persuasion
largely does not hold up, and the *identifiable victim effect* has now failed a preregistered
replication. Concreteness earning **belief** is well supported. Concreteness earning **persuasion**
or **generosity** is not. `slop-patterns.md`'s witness-detail doctrine survives, but its
justification has to change from "specific detail moves people" to "specific detail is believed,
and generic detail marks the writer as absent." Those are different claims and only the second one
is safe.

### 1.6 Negativity increases news consumption and news sharing — but read the discrete rows

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Robertson, Pröllochs, Schwarzenegger, Pärnamets, Van Bavel & Feuerriegel (2023), *Nature Human Behaviour* 7(5):812–822, [10.1038/s41562-023-01538-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-023-01538-4). **Full text via PMC** | **22,743 randomized controlled trials** (Upworthy Research Archive): ~105,000 headline variations, ~5.7M clicks, 370M+ impressions. **Causal — headlines were randomized** | Each additional **negative** word: **+2.3% CTR** (β = 0.015, SE 0.001, 99% CI 0.013–0.018). Each additional **positive** word: **−1.0% CTR** (β = −0.008, 99% CI −0.010 to −0.006). Discrete emotions: **sadness β = +0.006** (p < .001), **joy β = −0.009**, **fear β = −0.007**, **anger β = 0.000, p = .666 (null)** |
| Watson, van der Linden, Watson & Stillwell (2024), *Scientific Reports* 14:21592, [10.1038/s41598-024-71263-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-71263-z). **Full text via PMC** | **95,282 articles** (Guardian, Daily Mail, NYT, NY Post, 2019–2021) × **579,182,075 Facebook and Twitter posts**; doubly robust estimation, 1,000 bootstrap samples. **Correlational** | Negative articles received **~1.91× more shares** overall. Facebook +98% posts; Twitter +34% tweets. Larger for global news, local news, family/home, and political topics |

**Verdict: Established** for the headline-level direction, in news.

**Three cautions before anyone builds on this:**

1. Robertson measures **clicks, not shares**. Consumption and transmission are different outcomes
   and the plugin should never conflate them — `ranking-signals.md` already treats views and
   reshares as separate predictions.
2. **Anger was exactly null** in the randomized data. The single most-cited "high-arousal negative
   emotion" result did not move clicks at all when headlines were randomized.
3. **Sadness was positive** in both the randomized click data and the Facebook sharing data. See §4.2.
4. This is news. A creator posting concert or lifestyle footage is not in the news distribution
   system, and "add negative words" is a terrible instruction to derive from it. It is reported
   here as background, not as a rule.

### 1.7 The closest thing to evidence about *video* sharing — and it contradicts the email findings

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Tellis, MacInnis, Tirunillai & Zhang (2019), *Journal of Marketing* 83(4), [10.1177/0022242919841034](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022242919841034). **Full abstract** | **Two independent field studies** of video ad sharing across multiple social platforms, coding **11 emotions and 60+ ad characteristics**, plus a **third study on an entirely independent holdout sample** | • **Information-focused content has a significantly NEGATIVE effect on sharing**, except in risky contexts<br>• Positive emotions of **amusement, excitement, inspiration, and warmth** positively affect sharing<br>• Drama elements — **surprise, plot, and characters** (babies, animals, celebrities) — arouse the emotions that drive sharing<br>• **Prominent brand placement hurts sharing** (early vs. late, long vs. short, persistent vs. pulsing)<br>• Emotional ads shared more on general platforms; informational ads more on LinkedIn<br>• Sharing greatest at **moderate length (1.2–1.7 minutes)** |

**Verdict: Established for video ads**, with a holdout validation — a higher bar than most of this
literature clears.

> **Caption implication, and this is the biggest single actionable split in the file.** Berger &
> Milkman found **practical utility +30%** on emailed newspaper articles. Tellis et al. found
> **information-focused content significantly reduces sharing** of video. Both are competent
> studies. They disagree because the medium and the act differ: forwarding a useful article *is*
> the helpful act; a useful video gets **saved**, not sent.
>
> This maps directly onto a row `2026-08-20-diagnostics.md` already recommends — *"saves high,
> sends low → fine for craft work."* That row now has an outside evidence base. Utility is a real
> Reward and a weak Share driver. The plugin should say so.

## 2. What is suggestive but weaker

### 2.1 Berger & Milkman (2012) itself — the canonical paper, read in full, with its limits stated

Berger & Milkman, "What Makes Online Content Viral?", *Journal of Marketing Research* 49(2):192–205,
[10.1509/jmr.10.0353](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.10.0353). **Read in full** from the author's
posted PDF.

**Study 1 — archival.** All articles on nytimes.com between **30 August and 30 November 2008**;
**6,956 articles**; 20% made the most-emailed list. Positivity and emotionality coded automatically
(LIWC, 7,630-word list). Specific emotions coded by **three human raters each** on a 5-point scale
(all interrater α > .70) on a **random subsample of n = 2,566**. The other 4,390 articles were
**assigned the mean (zero after standardization) with a dummy control** — a standard imputation,
but it means roughly **63% of the sample carries no specific-emotion measurement at all**. Model 6
restricts to the coded articles and reports similar results.

Effect sizes, from the paper's Figure 2 — % change in fitted probability of making the list for a
+1 SD increase:

| Characteristic | Effect |
|---|---|
| Anger | **+34%** |
| Awe | **+30%** |
| Practical value | **+30%** |
| Interest | **+25%** |
| Anxiety | **+21%** |
| **Time at top of home page** (for scale) | **+20%** |
| Emotionality | **+18%** |
| Surprise | **+14%** |
| Positivity | **+13%** |
| **Sadness** | **−16%** |

The paper's own comparison: a +1 SD increase in anger is "equivalent to spending an additional 2.9
hours as the lead story… nearly four times the average number of hours articles spend in that
position."

**Studies 2a, 2b and 3 — the lab experiments that carry the causal claim.**

| Study | N | Manipulation | Outcome measured |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2a | **49** | High vs. low amusement (Jimmy Dean sausage ad story) | 7-point *"how likely would you be to share"* — **intention, not behaviour** |
| 2b | **45** | High vs. low anger (United Airlines guitar story) | same |
| 3 | **47** | High vs. low sadness (9/11 injury vs. office-stairs injury) | same |

All three used a three-item arousal index and **Sobel tests** for mediation.

**Verdict: Suggestive, not Established.** Specifically:

- The archival result is large-N, well controlled, and holds up — **for emailing New York Times
  articles in autumn 2008**.
- The **causal** claim rests on three experiments of **N = 45–49**, measuring **self-reported
  sharing intention** for a **single stimulus per condition**, with **Sobel mediation**. By 2026
  standards that is three underpowered single-item studies. Scholz et al. (§1.1) found neural
  signals outpredicted "commonly used self-report measures of sharing intentions" — i.e. the exact
  dependent variable these three experiments use.
- Berger & Milkman is *the* citation everyone reaches for. It deserves to be cited. It does not
  deserve to be treated as settled, and its lab arm is the weakest part of it.

### 2.2 The arousal mechanism specifically has FAILED to replicate

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Prowten, Walker, London, Pearce, Napoli, Chenevert, Clevenger & Smith (2024), *Psychological Science* 35(9), [10.1177/09567976241257255](https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976241257255). **Abstract + institutional summary; publisher blocked full text** | **Two replications, N = 111 and N = 160**, of Berger (2011) — original **N = 40** — using the same arousal manipulation, with the sharing measure updated to social media | Neither study found evidence that physiological arousal increased willingness to share news stories. Verbatim: "in contrast to Berger (2011), we did not find evidence that increased physiological arousal corresponded to an increase in the number of articles shared" |

**Verdict: Refuted**, for the mechanism. Note precisely what this does and does not hit:

- It **replicates Berger (2011)**, the *incidental arousal* study — not Berger & Milkman's archival
  regression.
- It does **not** show anger or awe fail to predict sharing. It shows the **explanation** — "these
  emotions work *because* they raise physiological activation" — did not hold with 2.8–4× the
  original sample.
- Combined with Paletz et al. (§1.3), where the arousal model lost outright to a discrete-emotion
  model on 4,000+ posts, **the arousal account should be dropped from the plugin's reasoning
  entirely.** Keep the emotions. Drop the theory that ranks them by activation.

This is the most important single thing in this file, because "high-arousal emotion drives shares"
is exactly the kind of confident, mechanistic, wrong-sounding-when-wrong claim the plugin's gate
exists to catch — and it is currently implicit in `arrrsr.md`'s emotion list ordering.

### 2.3 Partial and failed replication of Berger & Milkman in other channels

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Heimbach & Hinz (2016), *International Journal of Research in Marketing* 33(3):695–701, [10.1016/j.ijresmar.2016.02.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijresmar.2016.02.004). **Abstract only — publisher returned 403** | German news magazine most-emailed list, extended to three additional communication channels | Replicated the most-emailed setting, but **could not replicate all of Berger & Milkman's effects**, and the emotion-dimension findings were **irregular across channels**. Also found the positivity–virality relationship is **non-linear, inverted-U** — extreme positivity does *not* keep helping |

**Verdict: Suggestive.** Two things worth carrying: effects are **channel-specific** (which is
Berger's own 2025 review position), and **more positive is not monotonically better**.

### 2.4 Moral-emotional language — a famous result that did not survive re-analysis

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Brady, Wills, Jost, Tucker & Van Bavel (2017), *PNAS* 114(28):7313–7318, [10.1073/pnas.1618923114](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1618923114). **Full abstract** | **n = 563,312 tweets** on three polarizing moral/political issues | Each additional **moral-emotional word** increased diffusion by **~20%**. Effect was bounded by group membership — stronger within networks than between |
| Burton, Cruz & Hahn (2021), *Nature Human Behaviour* 5(12):1629–1635, [10.1038/s41562-021-01133-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-021-01133-5). **Full abstract** | Out-of-sample prediction, model comparison, specification curve analysis on the same phenomenon | Verbatim: "**the moral contagion model performs no better than an implausible XYZ contagion model.** This highlights the risks of using purely correlational evidence from large observational datasets" |

**Verdict: Refuted as stated.** Do not cite the +20%-per-moral-word figure.

This is also a **general warning about the entire genre**, including two studies cited approvingly
in §1.6: a large-N correlational text-feature model of social media diffusion can produce a clean
significant coefficient for a feature that carries no real predictive signal. Watson et al. (§1.6)
is in this genre. Robertson et al. is not, because its headlines were randomized.

### 2.5 Specificity: the two classic supports are weaker than the plugin assumes

The plugin's witness-detail doctrine is usually justified in popular writing by two findings. Both
are in trouble.

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| **"One specific person beats a statistic"** (identifiable victim effect) | **Refuted at the standard of a preregistered replication.** Lee & Feeley (2016), *Social Influence*, [10.1080/15534510.2016.1216891](https://doi.org/10.1080/15534510.2016.1216891) — meta-analysis of **41 effects from 22 experiments** — found a "significant yet modest" effect of **r = .05**. Maier, Wong & Feldman (2023), *Collabra: Psychology* 9(1), [10.1525/collabra.90203](https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.90203) — **preregistered, N = 1,004** — found **no empirical support** (ω = .000, 95% CI [.000, .003]), noted the three highest-powered studies in Lee & Feeley's dataset show near-zero effects (one with 12,802 participants at r = 0.004), and re-analysed the meta-analysis with robust Bayesian methods indicating publication bias. They also cite Moche (2022) failing in **6 of 7 well-powered studies** and a failed field experiment (Lesner & Rasmussen 2014). **Read in full.** Caveat the authors themselves raise: their study used *hypothetical* donations, not real money |
| **"Vivid presentation is more persuasive"** | **Refuted as a general claim since 1982.** Taylor & Thompson (1982), *Psychological Review* 89(2):155–191 — reviewed the experimental literature and found "little support"; also found the proposed mechanisms (memorability, imageability, affective impact) themselves problematic. **Abstract only** |

**Verdict on the doctrine itself: it survives, on different grounds.** What the evidence *does*
support:

- Concrete language → **judged more probably true** (Hansen & Wänke, §1.5, four experiments).
- Verisimilitude and imaginable plot → **transportation** (van Laer meta-analysis, ρ = .27 and .29).
- Transportation → affective response **ρ = .57**, attitudes **ρ = .44**, and **reduced critical
  thoughts ρ = −.20** (van Laer).

So: **specific detail earns belief and absorption. It does not reliably move donations or
persuade.** `slop-patterns.md` is making the first claim — its primary test is "could someone who
was not there have written this?", which is a *presence* claim, not a persuasion claim. That is the
defensible version, and it should be stated that way rather than drifting toward "specificity is
more moving."

### 2.6 Individual emotions, one at a time, and what each is actually evidenced for

Nearly all of these establish what the emotion *does to a person*, not that it *causes sharing*.
That distinction is the whole point of this section.

| Emotion | Best evidence found | What it establishes | Sharing evidence? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Awe** | Piff, Dietze, Feinberg, Stancato & Keltner (2015), *JPSP* 108(6):883–99, [10.1037/pspi0000018](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000018) — **5 studies, N = 2,078**, including a naturalistic induction in a grove of towering trees | Awe → "small self" → prosociality, generosity, ethical decisions. Strong, multi-method | **No.** Sharing evidence for awe is Berger & Milkman's single archival coefficient (+30%) |

## 3. What is folklore

Every item here appears constantly in creator-marketing writing. None traces to a study I could
name with a method. Refuse all of them, and refuse the softened versions too.

| Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **"94% of people share to be helpful"** and its siblings (49% / 68% / 73% / 84%) | Traces to *The Psychology of Sharing*, New York Times Customer Insight Group with Latitude Research, **2011**. It is a real study with a stated design — ethnographies, a one-week sharing panel, and a survey of **2,500 self-identified medium-to-heavy online sharers** — but it is **proprietary marketing research, never peer reviewed, with no published statistical methodology**, on a self-selected sharer population, from 2011. Cite it as an industry survey if at all. **Never as psychology.** The peer-reviewed answer to the same question is Berger (2014/2025) — five functions, "predominantly self-serving" — which is close to the *opposite* framing |
| **"Content with emotion gets 3× the shares"** | No source. The nearest real numbers are Berger & Milkman's **+13% to +34% odds per SD** on a 2008 newspaper email list, and Watson et al.'s **1.91×** for negative news links. Neither is "3×," neither is about video, and neither is about "emotion" generically |
| **"Videos with on-screen text get 28% higher engagement / 2.3× more engagement / 28% higher completion"** | **Fabricated attribution.** Repeated across the short-form-SEO blog genre. No platform page and no study. Same failure mode as the "YouTube confirms captioned Shorts rank 23% higher" claim already caught in [`2026-08-20-hardening.md` §A.3](2026-08-20-hardening.md). **This one matters most for this plugin, because on-screen caption text is the creator's whole format** — and there is no evidence base for it either way |
| **"High-arousal emotions drive sharing"** | Was a real theory. **Failed replication** at N = 111 and N = 160 (Prowten et al. 2024) and **lost a head-to-head model comparison** on 4,000+ posts (Paletz et al. 2023). Demote from mechanism to historical footnote |
| **"Sadness kills sharing"** | Berger & Milkman found −16% on 2008 NYT emails. Two much later and larger studies found sadness **positively** associated with sharing (Paletz 2023) and with clicks (Robertson 2023, randomized). See §4.2 |
| **"Relatable content works because of mirror neurons"** | No. Mirror-neuron explanations of social media engagement have no supporting study. The mechanism with actual evidence is **self-referential encoding** (Symons & Johnson 1997 meta-analysis + 2025 replications) |
| **"People share to be the first to know / for social currency"** as an established finding | "Social currency" is a popular-book coinage, not a measured construct. The measured version is **impression management**, one of Berger's five functions. Use that name |
| **"Storytelling increases engagement by X%"** | The real meta-analytic numbers exist and are modest — transportation → attitudes ρ = .44, intentions ρ = .31, and the storyteller-controlled antecedents are all ρ ≤ .29 (van Laer et al. 2014). Any round percentage claim about storytelling is invented |
| **"One person's story beats a statistic"** | See §2.5. **Preregistered N = 1,004 replication found nothing** |

Three need correcting.

### 4.1 What is confirmed — and can now carry a citation

| `arrrsr.md` line | Status | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| "**Sharing is identity, not enjoyment.** People forward what says something about them to the specific person they send it to." | **Confirmed** | Scholz et al. 2017 (117,611 real shares); Chan et al. 2023 (preregistered, two countries); Berger 2014/2025; Tamir & Mitchell 2012 |
| "A share is a stronger signal than a like because it costs something — it stakes a small claim about the sender's taste" | **Confirmed, and the cost is real** | Berger & Heath 2008: people abandon tastes to avoid signalling an unwanted identity. Misidentification is an actual cost, not a metaphor |
| "**The send test:** who forwards this, to whom specifically, and what does sending it say about the sender?" | **Confirmed — keep verbatim** | It is a plain-language restatement of Scholz/Chan's two inputs: self-relevance and social relevance |
| "**discrete emotions predict sharing; positivity alone does not**" | **Confirmed, and stronger than written** | Paletz et al. 2023 — discrete-emotion models beat valence models **and arousal models** head to head on 4,000+ posts |
| **Relevance** stage: "the viewer recognizes themselves… not just that the subject is generic but specific" | **Confirmed, with a named mechanism** | Self-referential encoding (Symons & Johnson 1997 meta-analysis). Worth naming in the file — it converts a taste judgment into a testable one |

### 4.2 The sadness entry — the correction the brief asked about

**Short answer: the taxonomy entry is fine; the belief behind it is wrong.**

`arrrsr.md` lists sadness last in an unordered list, which is harmless in itself. The problem is the
**unwritten assumption** anyone reaching for Berger & Milkman will bring: *sadness suppresses
sharing.* Three data points:

| Source | Medium | Sadness result |
|---|---|---|
| Berger & Milkman 2012 | Emailing NYT articles, 2008 | **−16%** odds of making the most-emailed list |
| Paletz et al. 2023, *Science Advances* | Facebook posts, Poland + Lithuania, 4,000+ posts, 20+ annotated emotions | **Positively associated with sharing** |
| Robertson et al. 2023, *Nature Human Behaviour* | **Randomized** headline A/B tests, 105k variations | **Sadness β = +0.006, p < .001** on click-through — while **joy and fear were negative and anger was null** |

The 2012 result is one archival regression, on one publication, in one channel, in one country, in
one three-month window, with 63% of the sample's specific-emotion values imputed. The two later
results come from a head-to-head model comparison and from randomized experiments. **The direction
should not be carried forward as a rule.**

**Recommended edit to `arrrsr.md`:** add one line under the emotional-payload taxonomy —

> This list is **unordered**. No emotion here is established as stronger than the others for
> short-form video, and the widely repeated claim that sadness suppresses sharing comes from a
> single 2008 study of newspaper *emails*; two larger and later studies found the opposite. Pick
> the emotion the footage actually carries.

### 4.3 What is missing from the taxonomy

| Add | Why | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Being moved / heartwarming** | The **only** emotion besides amusement and anger that a head-to-head emotion-theory comparison found associated with sharing. `arrrsr.md` has no entry for it — "awe" and "hope" both miss it. This is the "sudden intensification of closeness" feeling: a reunion, a stranger's kindness, a crowd singing a line together | Paletz 2023 (sharing); Zickfeld et al. 2019, *Emotion*, 19 nations / 15 languages / N = 3,542 (construct) |
| **Inspiration** and **warmth** | Two of the four emotions that positively predicted sharing in the best-matched medium available (video), across two field studies and a holdout sample | Tellis et al. 2019, *Journal of Marketing* |
| **Pride / vindication** | *Not added.* No study found. Listing it would be inventing a category |

### 4.4 The Reward table's four value types — one needs a medium caveat

The four types (Utility, Entertainment, Identity, Connection) are a sound working taxonomy and I
found nothing contradicting them. One row needs a warning:

> | Utility | A technique, setting, or fact they can use |

**Utility is a strong Reward and a weak Share driver in video.** Berger & Milkman found practical
value +30% for *emailing an article*; Tellis et al. found information-focused content
**significantly reduces sharing** of *video*. A useful clip gets **saved**, not sent — which is
exactly the "saves high, sends low" pattern `2026-08-20-diagnostics.md` already recommends treating
as normal for craft work.

**Recommended edit:** one line under the value table —

> Utility reliably earns **saves**; it does not reliably earn **sends**. In video specifically,
> information-focused content has been found to *reduce* sharing. Pair Utility with a second value
> type when the goal is a send.

### 4.5 Two smaller notes

- **Anger / injustice** should carry a caution. It is Berger & Milkman's largest coefficient
  (+34%), but it was **exactly null** in randomized headline experiments (Robertson 2023) and the
  famous moral-emotional-language result did not survive out-of-sample re-analysis (Burton et al.

## 5. Drop-in draft: `references/relatability.md`

The deliverable. Written as instructions an agent executes, matching the register of
`slop-patterns.md` and `hooks.md`. Every rule carries the finding it rests on, in square brackets,
pointing back to a section of this file. Nothing here duplicates `hooks.md` (which owns openings)
or `slop-patterns.md` (which owns the generic test) — this file owns **why a line makes a viewer
feel seen, and what makes it worth forwarding.**

Proposed length ~95 lines, so no table of contents is required under the skill-authoring rule in
`2026-08-20-hardening.md` §2.1 item 15.

> # Relatability and sharing
>
> Why a line makes a viewer feel seen, and what makes a post worth forwarding. `hooks.md` owns the
> opening; `slop-patterns.md` owns the generic test; this file owns the middle and the caption.
>
> Every rule below traces to a named study. The evidence, its sample sizes, and its limits are in
> `research/2026-08-20-sharing-psychology.md`. **Where the evidence stops, this file says so** —
> a labelled bet is useful, an unlabelled one is a lie.
>
> ## The one rule
>
> **Write the line the viewer would say about themselves, not the line you would say about the
> footage.**
>
> Sharing is not enjoyment. What predicts a share is whether the content is **self-relevant** and
> **socially relevant** — whether forwarding it says something the sender wants said about them, to
> the person they send it to. [Established: fMRI linked to 117,611 real shares, replicated
> preregistered across two countries — Scholz et al. 2017 *PNAS*; Chan et al. 2023 *PNAS*. Word of
> mouth serves impression management and social bonding, and is "predominantly self-serving" —
> Berger 2014 *JCP*.]
>
> Everything else in this file is a way of executing that rule.
>
> ## Aim the sentence at the viewer, not at the world
>
> A line the viewer has to check against their own memory is processed differently from a line
> that describes a category. This is the mechanism under "relatable," and it is a fifty-year-old
> memory finding, not a vibe. [Established: meta-analysis, Symons & Johnson 1997 *Psychological
> Bulletin*; replicated with fMRI, N = 83 and N = 54, 2025.]
>
> | Describes a category (semantic) | Forces a check against the self (self-referential) |
> |---|---|
> | "Everyone's phone struggles in low light." | "You've got forty photos from that night and you can't tell which one is your friend." |
> | "Parking at these things is a nightmare." | "You know the exact second you gave up and just took the ticket." |
> | "It's hard to enjoy a show while filming it." | "I watched the whole encore through a five-inch screen and I still don't know what the last song was." |
> | "Small venues have better atmosphere." | "The whole room knew the words to a song that has 11,000 plays." |
>
> **The test:** does the line require the reader to retrieve a memory in order to parse it? If it
> can be understood without one, it is a description.
>
> **The failure to avoid:** a line so general that everyone technically qualifies — "we've all been
> there" — is not self-referential, it is the absence of a referent. Recognition needs a *specific*
> situation the reader either has or hasn't been in. Narrow beats broad here, every time.
>
> ## Say it in first or second person, about one occasion
>
> Three moves, in order of how much they change a line:
>
> 1. **One occasion, not a habit.** "the encore last Tuesday," not "encores."
> 2. **A person in it** — you, or the viewer. Not "one" and not "people."
> 3. **A detail that fixes it in time** — a number, an object, a physical action.
>
> Concrete language is judged **more probably true than abstract language carrying the identical
> claim**, mediated by perceived vividness. [Established: four experiments, Hansen & Wänke 2010
> *PSPB*.] And of everything a storyteller controls, only two things reliably increase absorption:
> an **imaginable** plot (ρ = .29) and **verisimilitude** — believability, "the likelihood that
> story events may actually happen" (ρ = .27). [Established: meta-analysis of 132 effect sizes,
> van Laer et al. 2014 *JCR*.]
>
> **What this does not license.** Specific detail buys **belief** and **absorption**. It does not
> reliably buy persuasion or generosity — the classic "vividness effect" mostly failed to replicate
> (Taylor & Thompson 1982), and the "one victim beats a statistic" effect failed a preregistered
> N = 1,004 replication (Maier et al. 2023). So never argue that a detail is *moving*. Argue that
> it is **believed**, and that its absence marks the writer as someone who wasn't there. That is
> the claim `slop-patterns.md` is actually making.
>
> ## Name one emotion, and pick it from what the evidence supports
>
> Discrete emotions predict sharing. Valence does not, and **arousal does not** — a head-to-head
> comparison of all three theories on 4,000+ annotated posts found the discrete-emotion model won.
> [Established: Paletz et al. 2023 *Science Advances*.] Do not rank emotions by intensity or
> "activation"; that theory failed to replicate at N = 111 and N = 160. [Prowten et al. 2024
> *Psychological Science*.]
>
> | Emotion | Evidence that it drives sharing | Use it |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Amusement** | Strongest cross-medium support: Facebook posts, video ads, and lab | Freely |
> | **Being moved / heartwarming** | Facebook sharing (Paletz); construct validated across 19 nations | Freely. This is the reunion, the stranger's kindness, the crowd carrying a line |
> | **Inspiration**, **warmth**, **excitement** | Two independent field studies of **video** sharing plus a holdout sample (Tellis et al. 2019) — the best-matched medium available | Freely |
> | **Recognition ("this is exactly my experience")** | **No direct sharing study exists.** Inferred from self-referential encoding + the self-relevance input to share-value | **The plugin's central bet. Label it as a bet, act on it anyway** |
> | **Surprise** | Video ads (drama element); one archival study (+14%) | Yes |
> | **Awe** | One archival study (+30%). The awe literature itself is strong but measures prosociality, not sharing | Yes, without the confident theory |
> | **Anger / injustice** | One archival study (+34%), but **exactly null** in randomized headline experiments, and the famous moral-language result did not survive re-analysis | Only when it is genuinely the story. Never manufactured |
> | **Sadness** | **Contested.** One 2008 email study says it suppresses sharing; two larger later studies (one randomized) found the opposite | Do not avoid it on the strength of the old finding |
> | **Nostalgia** | **No sharing study found.** What is established is that nostalgia works through **narrative** — it needs a story about a transition, not just an old object | Use, but build it as a story |
> | **Curiosity** | Owned by `hooks.md`. An opened gap that never closes reads as a tactic and **costs** shares | Only with the close attached |
>
> **One dominant emotion per piece.** Two named emotions is a piece that decided nothing.
>
> ## Utility earns saves, not sends
>
> A technique, setting, or fact is a real Reward — it is not a Share driver. In video specifically,
> **information-focused content significantly reduces sharing**. [Established: two independent field
> studies plus a holdout sample, Tellis et al. 2019 *Journal of Marketing*.] This is why a genuinely
> useful post can show high saves and near-zero sends, and why that is not a defect
> (`references/arrrsr.md`, and the diagnostic table in `ff-strategy`).
>
> **If a send is the goal, pair Utility with a second value type.** The technique plus the moment it
> cost you. The setting plus what you got wrong first.
>
> ## What reads as a tactic, and what it costs
>
> The mechanism is specific and worth stating, because it explains why bait is *worse than neutral*
> rather than merely ineffective: a reader who infers **manipulative intent** derogates the
> **source** — judges the author less competent and less trustworthy — and then doesn't share.
> [Established: controlled experiment plus 19,386 articles across 27 publishers with Twitter share
> data; clickbait articles got **far fewer** shares, with large effect sizes. Mukherjee, Dutta &
> De Bruyn 2022 *JAMS*.]
>
> Note what the same study found in the other direction: **high-emotional headlines were shared
> more.** The problem is never emotional intensity. It is visible engineering.
>
> | Reads as a tactic | The same idea, without the tell |
> |---|---|
> | "Tag someone who needs to see this 👀" | Nothing. If the send test is passed, the send happens; if it isn't, asking won't fix it |
> | "Wait for it…" | Name what is coming and stay for the reason it is worth waiting: "the lighting guy did something at 0:09 I've never seen anyone do live" |
> | "This changed everything for me." | "I stopped bringing the second lens after this night." |
> | "Comment your thoughts below!" | A real unresolved disagreement: "I think the amber grade is worse. Convince me." |
> | "Nobody talks about this." | "I've asked four people who shoot these and none of them do this, so I might be wrong." |
> | "POV: you're at the best show of your life" | "POV: it's song four and you've just realised you're going to be filming this instead of watching it" |
>
> **The false-intimacy version of the same failure.** Manufactured closeness — confiding a feeling
> the footage does not support, "you guys," an addressed vulnerability that costs nothing — is the
> same inference of engineered intent, applied to a relationship instead of a headline. There is no
> study measuring this specifically; it is grouped here because the mechanism is the same one and
> the plugin should treat it the same way. **Labelled: this row is an extension, not a finding.**
>
> ## Recurring format beats one interesting post
>
> Interesting things get **immediate** word of mouth but not **ongoing** word of mouth. Things that
> are **cued by the environment or publicly visible** get both — right away and over months.
> [Established: 300+ products, field experiment and lab experiment with real conversations, Berger
> & Schwartz 2011 *JMR*.]
>
> A recognizable recurring format is a cue. This is the evidence behind the **Relationship** stage
> in `arrrsr.md`, and the reason `ff-package` logs hook architectures — a format the audience can
> recognize is an asset; the same shape four posts running is a liability
> (`data/hooks-used.md`).
>
> ## Checklist before a caption ships
>
> 1. **Send test.** Who forwards this, to whom, and what does sending it say about them? Not
>    "someone might like this."
> 2. **Self-reference test.** Does at least one line require the reader to retrieve their own
>    memory to parse it?
> 3. **One emotion named**, from the table above, and it is the one the footage actually carries.
> 4. **One detail only presence supplies** (`slop-patterns.md`) — for belief, not for effect.
> 5. **No visible tactic.** Nothing that a reader could name as a move.
> 6. **If Utility is the only value type**, either accept it will be saved rather than sent, or add
>    a second.

1. `ff-package` must add `references/relatability.md` to its Step 0 read list and to its Reads
